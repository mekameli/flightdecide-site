The 3° rule: start down at 3 NM per 1,000 ft to lose, and fly groundspeed × 5 as your rate. Losing 6,000 ft means a top of descent 18 NM out; at 120 kt that is 600 fpm. It is the same geometry an ILS glideslope uses, and it is the difference between a descent you planned and a dive you apologized for.

How descent planning works

  • Time = altitude to lose ÷ rate. 6,000 ft at 500 fpm is 12 minutes.
  • Distance = groundspeed × time. 12 minutes at 120 kt is 24 NM: that is your top of descent.
  • Required rate (when the distance is fixed, like a crossing restriction) = altitude ÷ time available. The moment that number gets big, you are negotiating with physics.
  • Gradient = altitude ÷ distance, in ft/NM; ÷ 100 gives roughly the descent angle in degrees (a 318 ft/NM path is the standard 3°).

Two cockpit shortcuts worth memorizing: 3 × altitude-in-thousands = distance, and groundspeed × 5 = the 3° rate. This calculator is those rules with the rounding removed.

Typical POH best-glide figures (verify in your POH; weight and configuration matter): Cessna 152 and 172 around 9:1 at 60–68 KIAS, Piper Archer about 10:1, Cirrus SR22 roughly 9:1 at 88 KIAS, Diamond DA40 up to 11:1. From 5,000 ft AGL that is a 7 to 9 NM circle in still air.

How pilots use these numbers

  • Every arrival: compute the top of descent before engine start (altitude to lose × 3 NM). Starting down there at 500 fpm arrives at pattern altitude 2 to 3 miles out, no diving, no shock cooling, no ear complaints.
  • Crossing restrictions: "cross 10 west at 4,000" turns into a required rate in one calculation. If it comes out over about 1,000 fpm, ask for lower now or add track miles.
  • The glide circle habit: knowing your AGL height and ratio means always having a radius in mind. Route selection over hostile terrain (water, mountains, forest at night) is choosing altitudes that keep a landable spot inside that circle.
  • Engine-out drill: height ÷ 1,000 × ratio ÷ 6 ≈ NM of glide. Practicing that mental math is what makes the real one calm.
