The 1-2-3 rule: from 1 hour before to 1 hour after your ETA, the destination forecast must show at least a 2,000 ft ceiling and 3 SM visibility, or you must file an alternate. The alternate itself has to qualify: standard minima are 600-2 with a precision approach and 800-2 without one, unless the chart publishes different numbers. A TAF showing 1,500 broken at your ETA means you're filing an alternate.

Step 1: the 1-2-3 rule at the destination

14 CFR 91.169 lets you skip the alternate only when both of these hold for the window one hour either side of your ETA:

  • Forecast ceiling at least 2,000 ft above the airport elevation
  • Forecast visibility at least 3 statute miles

Read the TAF for the whole window, including TEMPO and PROB groups; a two-hour TEMPO of 1,000 overcast inside your window fails the test even if the prevailing line looks fine. And if the destination has no published instrument approach at all, the forecast is irrelevant: an alternate is required.

Step 2: standard alternate minimums

An airport qualifies as your alternate only if, at your ETA there, the forecast meets the alternate minimums for its approaches:

Best approach at the alternateRequired forecast at your ETA
Precision (ILS or equivalent)600 ft ceiling and 2 SM
Non-precision only800 ft ceiling and 2 SM
No instrument approachDescent from the MEA, approach, and landing in basic VFR

Two traps hide behind that table. First, many airports publish non-standard alternate minimums that replace 600-2 and 800-2; the approach chart carries the alternate-minimums symbol when they do, and some approaches are marked NA for alternate use entirely (often because the weather reporting or navaid monitoring goes away when the tower closes). Second, GPS: a non-WAAS installation may plan an RNAV (GPS) approach at the destination or the alternate, not both, and WAAS planning generally uses the LNAV line of minima. AIM 1-1-17 and 1-1-18 carry the details.

The fuel rule that comes with the alternate

Filing an alternate changes your fuel math. 14 CFR 91.167 requires enough fuel to fly to the destination, then to the alternate, then 45 minutes at normal cruise. Run the trip through the fuel and endurance calculator with the alternate leg included; a legal alternate you can't reach with reserves intact isn't an alternate.

Flying in Canada: stricter, and different numbers

Canada doesn't have a 1-2-3 escape hatch. CAR 602.122 requires an alternate on an IFR flight plan regardless of the destination forecast, with only narrow exceptions. The alternate weather minima also work differently: the Canada Air Pilot's alternate-minima table keys on how many usable approaches the airport has, not on a precision/non-precision split. If you fly both sides of the border, don't let the FAA numbers drive a Canadian plan; our Canadian weather briefing guide covers the sources.

Legal minimum vs. good plan

The 1-2-3 rule is a floor, not a strategy. A destination forecast of 2,100 and 3 passes the test and still leaves you one TEMPO group from an approach to minimums with no plan B. Plenty of instrument pilots file an alternate whenever the destination is below their personal VFR-ish numbers, and pick alternates with weather good enough to be boring. The personal minimums worksheet is where those numbers get written down.
