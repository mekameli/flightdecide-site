A METAR is an airport's weather observation in code: station, time, wind, visibility, weather, clouds, temperature and dew point, altimeter, then remarks. KBFI 261753Z 31015G24KT 10SM FEW045 22/09 A3005 is Boeing Field on the 26th at 17:53 UTC: wind 310° true at 15 knots gusting 24, visibility 10 miles or more, a few clouds at 4,500 ft, 22 °C with a 9 °C dew point, altimeter 30.05. It's an observation, not a forecast; the TAF tells you what's coming.

Reading a METAR, group by group

The groups always come in the same order, and a group that has nothing to report is left out rather than written as zero.

GroupExampleWhat it means
StationKBFIFour-letter ICAO identifier. K is the contiguous US, C is Canada, PA is Alaska.
Time261753ZDay of the month, then the time in UTC: the 26th at 17:53 UTC.
Wind31015G24KTFrom 310° true at 15 knots, gusting 24. VRB04KT is variable at 4; 00000KT is calm; 280V350 after the wind means the direction swings between the two.
Visibility1 1/2SMStatute miles, fractions included. M1/4SM is less than a quarter mile; 10SM is 10 or more.
Weather-SHRALight rain showers. A minus is light, no sign is moderate, a plus is heavy; VC means 5 to 10 miles from the station.
CloudsBKN014Cover and base in hundreds of feet above the ground: few, scattered, broken, overcast. VV002 is a sky obscured with 200 ft of vertical visibility.
TemperatureM05/M08Temperature and dew point in °C; M is minus. A spread of 2 °C or less is fog and low-cloud territory.
AltimeterA298729.87 inHg. ICAO reports give Q1012 in hectopascals instead.
RemarksRMK AO2 SLP127What didn't fit above: the station type, sea-level pressure, exact temperatures, peak wind, when precipitation began.

Ceiling and flight category

The ceiling is the lowest broken or overcast layer, or the vertical visibility when the sky is obscured. Few and scattered layers never count, however low they sit. The category is set by the worse of ceiling and visibility:

CategoryCeilingVisibility
VFRabove 3,000 ftand more than 5 SM
MVFR (marginal VFR)1,000 to 3,000 ftor 3 to 5 SM
IFR500 to 999 ftor 1 to under 3 SM
LIFR (low IFR)below 500 ftor under 1 SM

These are the Aviation Weather Center's chart categories. They describe the weather; they don't make a flight legal or sensible. An MVFR field with 2,000 overcast and 6 miles is legal VFR in most airspace and a poor place for a low-time pilot to go looking for the airport. Compare the numbers with your own personal minimums.

Remarks worth reading

  • AO1 or AO2: an automated station. AO2 has a precipitation discriminator and can tell rain from snow; AO1 can't.
  • SLP176: sea-level pressure 1017.6 hPa. Put a 10 in front below 500, a 9 above it.
  • T02220094: temperature 22.2 °C and dew point 9.4 °C. A 1 in place of the 0 means minus.
  • PK WND 33032/1731: peak wind 330° at 32 knots, at 17:31 UTC. Worth a look when the main group says 18 knots.
  • RAB33, TSB25: rain began at 33 past the hour, thunder at 25. E is ended.
  • PRESRR, PRESFR: pressure rising or falling rapidly, often a front or a storm nearby.
  • $: the station needs maintenance. Treat the numbers with suspicion.

Canadian METARs add the cloud types and how much of the sky each covers, in eighths: SC1AC5 is stratocumulus 1/8 and altocumulus 5/8. Reports outside North America use metres for visibility (9999 is 10 km or more), hectopascals for the altimeter, and CAVOK when visibility is 10 km or more with no cloud below 5,000 ft and no significant weather.

Common Questions

True. The wind in a written METAR or TAF is in degrees true. Winds on ATIS, AWOS or ASOS and from the tower are magnetic, like the runway numbers. Where variation is large, correct a METAR wind before you run it through the crosswind calculator.

Visibility of 10 statute miles or more. US reports stop at 10 SM, so 10SM means at least 10. Canadian observers report higher values such as 15SM, and ICAO reports use metres, where 9999 means 10 km or more.

A ceiling is the lowest broken (BKN) or overcast (OVC) layer, or a vertical visibility (VV) into an obscured sky. Few and scattered layers cover less than half the sky, so they never make a ceiling. A scattered layer at 800 ft can still put a cloud exactly where you want to be on final.
