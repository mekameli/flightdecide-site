Is an App a Legal Weather Briefing?

FlightDecide vs 1800wxbrief — the safety-first answer. No app we make (or anyone makes) is an official weather briefing. Here's what 91.103 actually requires, what AC 91-92 permits, and where a decision-support app genuinely helps.

Home › Compare › FlightDecide vs Official Briefing

FlightDecide is not an official weather briefing, and it doesn't try to be. 14 CFR 91.103 requires you to become familiar with all available information before every flight. The established ways to satisfy and document that are an official briefing through Leidos Flight Service (1800wxbrief.com or 1-800-992-7433) or a documented self-briefing using FAA/NWS primary sources per AC 91-92. FlightDecide is a decision layer that sits before and alongside that briefing — never in place of it.

What 91.103 actually requires

The regulation is short and worth reading directly. 14 CFR 91.103 says each pilot in command shall, before beginning a flight, "become familiar with all available information concerning that flight," and it specifically calls out weather reports and forecasts, fuel requirements, alternatives if the flight cannot be completed as planned, known traffic delays, runway lengths, and takeoff and landing distance data for the aircraft.

Notice what the rule does not say: it doesn't name a phone number, a website, or an app. It sets an information standard, not a channel. That's why the practical question isn't "which app is legal" — it's "can you show you actually became familiar with all available information, using sources that are complete and current?" That's where the official briefing and AC 91-92 come in.

What an official briefing gives you

Flight Service in the contiguous U.S. is operated by Leidos. You reach it at 1800wxbrief.com or by phone at 1-800-WX-BRIEF (1-800-992-7433). An official briefing gives you things no third-party app fully replicates:

  • Standard, abbreviated, and outlook briefings. A standard briefing is the full package for a flight departing within the next several hours; an abbreviated briefing updates or supplements information you already have; an outlook briefing covers departures six or more hours out.
  • Adverse conditions first. The briefing structure leads with what should stop you — SIGMETs, AIRMETs, TFRs, and other hazards along your route.
  • A logged record tied to your tail number. The briefing is recorded and associated with your N-number. If a question ever arises about whether you met your 91.103 obligation, that log is your documentation.
  • TFR currency. Flight Service is in the primary distribution chain for temporary flight restrictions — the category of information where staleness has the sharpest consequences.
  • A human briefer's judgment on request. On the phone, you can ask a briefer "what would you want to know about this route today?" — an option no automated product offers.

What AC 91-92 says about self-briefing

The FAA's AC 91-92 (Pilot's Guide to a Preflight Briefing) recognizes that pilots may self-brief rather than call Flight Service, and describes how to do it well. Its emphasis, fairly summarized:

  • Use approved primary sources. FAA and NWS products — aviationweather.gov, FAA NOTAM services, TFR listings — not screenshots of screenshots.
  • Follow a systematic process. A repeatable structure that covers the same categories a standard briefing does, every time, so a missing item is conspicuous rather than invisible.
  • Keep documentation. Retain a record of what you reviewed and when. A self-briefing you can't evidence is much weaker — practically and, if it ever matters, legally — than one you can.

Two cautions, because precision matters here. First, AC 91-92 is an advisory circular — guidance, not regulation; the binding requirement remains 91.103 itself. Second, "the FAA allows self-briefing" is not the same as "any app session counts as a briefing." The AC's acceptance is conditioned on primary sources, a systematic process, and documentation. If you self-brief casually, you haven't done what the AC describes.

Where FlightDecide fits

FlightDecide is decision support — a structured second opinion you run before or alongside the official briefing, not a substitute for it. Its raw data comes from aviationweather.gov and FAA NOTAM sources, and it does three things a briefing doesn't:

  • It scores, rather than recites. Eight risk categories — ceilings, visibility, winds, NOTAMs, fuel, takeoff/landing performance, weight and balance, and more — evaluated for your specific flight window against your specific aircraft's POH numbers, rolled into a GO / CAUTION / NO GO advisory.
  • It holds you to your own minimums. Your personal ceiling, visibility, and wind limits are inputs to the score. This directly targets the classic failure mode a briefing can't fix: reading a marginal TAF optimistically because you've already told the family you're coming.
  • Every AI statement is checkable. The narrative briefing is displayed next to the raw METARs, TAFs, and NOTAMs it was generated from — you can verify any claim against the source product. And its NOTAM analysis is explicit about the difference between "queried the source, found zero" and "coverage gap — we couldn't check," so silence is never dressed up as safety.

What it is not: an official briefing, a logged 91.103 record, a TFR authority, or a decision-maker. The verdict is advisory; the decision is yours.

A recommended workflow

  1. Days out: use FlightDecide and your planning apps to shape the plan — route, fuel, alternates, and an early read on whether the weather trend supports the trip at all.
  2. Day of flight: get an official briefing from Leidos Flight Service (or conduct a deliberate, documented AC 91-92 self-brief from primary sources). Then re-run FlightDecide close to departure so the score reflects the current METARs, TAFs, and NOTAMs — and compare its category-by-category read against what the briefing told you. Disagreement between the two is a signal to dig into the raw data, not to pick the answer you prefer.
  3. Final authority: the pilot in command, under 14 CFR 91.3. No briefing, briefer, or app makes the go/no-go decision. A structured framework helps — ours is written up in the go/no-go decision framework guide.
