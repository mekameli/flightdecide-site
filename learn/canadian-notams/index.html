Canadian NOTAMs Explained

The ICAO format decoded line by line: the Q-line, the lettered items, EST and PERM, and where a Canadian NOTAM briefing actually comes from.

Home › Pilot Guides › Canadian NOTAMs

Canadian NOTAMs are written in the ICAO format: a numbered header, a coded Q-line, and lettered items A) through G). NAV CANADA completed the switch on October 10, 2019, aligning Canada with the international standard, and they are geographically referenced (coordinates plus a radius) so briefing tools can filter by where your route actually goes. Get them from CFPS at plan.navcanada.ca or a Flight Information Centre at 1-866-WX-BRIEF. Once you can read the Q-line and the A) through G) items, the rest is vocabulary.

What format are Canadian NOTAMs in?

Since October 10, 2019, every Canadian domestic NOTAM has been published in the ICAO format. Before that date, Canada ran its own domestic style, organized around NOTAM files. NAV CANADA's transition replaced both the format and the distribution model in one move: NOTAMs are now coded to the international standard, and each one carries its own coordinates and radius instead of living in a regional file.

That second change matters as much as the first. Under the old NOTAM-file system, you pulled the file for a region and read everything in it, relevant or not. A geographically referenced NOTAM can be matched against your actual route: a briefing tool draws your track, buffers it, and keeps only the NOTAMs whose circles intersect it. The format looks more cryptic than plain text, but it exists precisely so machines can do that filtering for you.

One wrinkle for cross-border pilots: the United States still uses its own domestic format for many NOTAMs. Fly from Vancouver to Seattle and you will read ICAO-format NOTAMs for the Canadian half and US-domestic-format NOTAMs for the American half of the same flight. The content categories are the same; the packaging is not. (For what to prioritize once you have the list, see what NOTAMs to check before a flight.)

Anatomy of an ICAO NOTAM, with a real-shaped example

Here is a realistic (fictional) Canadian NOTAM for a runway closure at Victoria:

H2537/26 NOTAMN
Q) CZVR/QMRLC/IV/NBO/A/000/999/4839N12325W005
A) CYYJ  B) 2607221300  C) 2607230100
D) DAILY 1300-0100
E) RWY 09/27 CLSD DUE TO CONSTRUCTION

Line by line:

  • H2537/26 NOTAMN: series letter H, NOTAM number 2537, year 2026. The suffix tells you what kind of message this is: NOTAMN is a new NOTAM, NOTAMR replaces an earlier one (the replaced number follows), and NOTAMC cancels one. If you see a NOTAMR, stop using the NOTAM it names.
  • Q) CZVR/QMRLC/IV/NBO/A/000/999/4839N12325W005: the qualifier line, decoded in the next section.
  • A) CYYJ: the location the NOTAM applies to, as an ICAO identifier (an aerodrome, or an FIR for en route items).
  • B) 2607221300: start of validity, in YYMMDDHHMM format, UTC. Here: July 22, 2026, at 1300Z.
  • C) 2607230100: end of validity, same format. Two flags to watch: EST after the time means the end is estimated and the NOTAM stays in force until replaced or cancelled, and PERM means the change is permanent and will migrate into the publications.
  • D) DAILY 1300-0100: the schedule, present only when the condition is intermittent within the B) to C) window. This is where "closed, but only during working hours" lives, and skipping it is how pilots avoid runways that are actually open.
  • E) RWY 09/27 CLSD DUE TO CONSTRUCTION: the text of the NOTAM, in heavily abbreviated but more or less readable English.
  • F) and G) (not present here): lower and upper altitude limits, used when the NOTAM describes airspace, such as a restricted area activation or an obstacle.

So this NOTAM reads: new NOTAM for Victoria; runway 09/27 is closed for construction each day from 1300Z to 0100Z, from July 22 to July 23, 2026. If you land at 0300Z, it does not apply to you. That is the time-window filtering that makes NOTAM briefings survivable.

How do you read the Q-line?

The Q-line is the machine-readable summary. Taking Q) CZVR/QMRLC/IV/NBO/A/000/999/4839N12325W005 field by field:

  • CZVR: the FIR (here, Vancouver).
  • QMRLC: the five-letter Q-code. The first letter is always Q; the next two identify the subject (MR is a runway) and the final two the condition (LC is closed). You do not need to memorize the code tables; you need to recognize that this pair is the fastest possible triage. A briefing full of QMRs and QFAs (aerodrome status) reads differently from one full of QRRs (restricted areas).
  • IV: traffic qualifier. I for IFR, V for VFR, IV for both.
  • NBO: purpose qualifiers. N flags it for the immediate attention of operators, B marks it for inclusion in pre-flight briefings, O means it concerns flight operations. M marks miscellaneous items that briefings may not display by default.
  • A: scope. A for aerodrome, E for en route, W for a warning (and combinations such as AE).
  • 000/999: lower and upper limits in hundreds of feet (flight levels). 000/999 is the default when altitude does not usefully bound the item.
  • 4839N12325W005: the coordinates and radius in nautical miles. This is the geographic reference that lets a briefing system decide whether the NOTAM intersects your route at all.

In day-to-day reading, the Q-code and the scope letter are the two fields worth a glance before you commit to reading item E). Everything else in the Q-line mostly serves the software.

How is the Canadian NOTAM system different from the FAA's?

AspectCanada (NAV CANADA)United States (FAA)
FormatICAO format for all domestic NOTAMs since October 2019US domestic format still used for many NOTAMs, alongside ICAO-format output
Temporary restrictionsNo TFR product: airspace restrictions issued via NOTAM, including activation of CYR restricted areasThe TFR, an FDC NOTAM with its own graphical list at tfr.faa.gov
RetrievalCFPS at plan.navcanada.ca, or a Flight Information Centre at 1-866-WX-BRIEFFAA NOTAM Search, or a Leidos briefing at 1800wxbrief.com / 1-800-WX-BRIEF

The traps run in both directions. A US pilot heading north may look for a TFR map that does not exist and miss a CYR activation buried in the NOTAM text; a Canadian pilot heading south may skim past US keyword formatting (RWY, NAV, OBST headers without a Q-line) expecting ICAO items. If your flight crosses the border, brief each side from its own system, or use a tool that queries both.

Where do you get Canadian NOTAMs?

  • CFPS (plan.navcanada.ca): NAV CANADA's Collaborative Flight Planning Services. Free account, NOTAM briefings by aerodrome or along a route, alongside weather. This is the authoritative self-serve source.
  • A Flight Information Centre: 1-866-WX-BRIEF within Canada connects you to a NAV CANADA FIC specialist for an interpreted briefing, useful when the picture is complicated or you want a second opinion.
  • Your EFB: most apps carry Canadian NOTAMs, but coverage and filtering vary by app; know where yours gets them and whether it applies your flight window.

NOTAMs are one leg of a Canadian preflight; the Canadian weather briefing guide covers the other, and both feed the same go/no-go decision.

The empty-briefing caveat

One habit worth keeping on either side of the border: an empty NOTAM list is only meaningful if the source was actually queried. A tool that fails to reach the NOTAM system and a tool that queried it and got zero results can render the same blank screen. FlightDecide handles this explicitly for Canadian aerodromes: it queries NAV CANADA's CFPS NOTAM data directly, filters to your flight window, and if the source cannot be reached it reports a coverage gap instead of a falsely clean list. It never claims "no NOTAMs" without evidence the source was queried. Whatever tool you use, make sure you know which of those two states an empty list represents.

Common Questions

The qualifier line: FIR, a five-letter Q-code for the subject and condition, traffic (I/V/IV), purpose (N, B, O, M), scope (A/E/W), lower and upper altitude limits, and coordinates with a radius. It mostly exists so briefing systems can filter NOTAMs automatically by location and relevance.

CFPS at plan.navcanada.ca for self-serve briefings by aerodrome or route, or call a NAV CANADA Flight Information Centre at 1-866-WX-BRIEF within Canada for an interpreted briefing. EFB apps also carry Canadian NOTAMs; verify how yours sources and filters them.

Not as a named product. Temporary restrictions in Canada are issued as NOTAMs, including activation of CYR restricted areas. The effect is similar to a TFR, but there is no separate graphical TFR list: you find them in the NOTAM briefing itself.
