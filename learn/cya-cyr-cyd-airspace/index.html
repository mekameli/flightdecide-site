CYA, CYR, CYD: Canadian Class F Airspace

What each designation means, the entry rules, the suffix letters that tell you what's happening inside, and how to find out whether a zone is active before you get there.

Home › Pilot Guides › CYA, CYR, CYD Airspace

Class F is Canada's special use airspace, and every zone is one of three things. A CYA is an advisory area: entry is at your discretion, but you are expected to stay out unless you are part of the activity. A CYR is a restricted area: entry requires permission from the user agency, full stop. A CYD is a danger area, used for restricted-type areas over international waters where Canada cannot legally forbid entry: going in is legal, but the hazard is real and the risk is yours. Learn those three rules and the letter after the zone number, and Class F stops being mysterious.

The decoder

DesignationMeaningEntry ruleExample activity

CYRRestricted areaProhibited without authorization from the user agencyMilitary ranges, sensitive sites
CYDDanger area (over international waters)Legal to enter; you accept the hazardLive firing or military activity offshore

Zone identifiers follow the pattern CYA 123, CYR 456, CYD 789, and CYA identifiers carry a bracketed suffix letter that tells you what actually goes on inside:

  • (A) acrobatic (aerobatics)
  • (F) aircraft test
  • (H) hang gliding
  • (M) military operations
  • (P) parachuting
  • (S) soaring
  • (T) training

The suffix changes how you think about the zone. CYA 187(T) near a busy flight school means climbing and descending trainers doing airwork; a (P) zone means canopies descending through your altitude; an (S) zone means gliders that are hard to see and can be anywhere from the surface to the cloud base. Same entry rule, very different threat picture.

Can I fly through a CYA?

Legally, yes. A CYA does not require a clearance or anyone's permission; entry is at the pilot's discretion. Practically, the expectation in Canada is that non-participating aircraft avoid active advisory areas, and that expectation exists for a good reason: the airspace was designated because a concentrated activity there is hazardous to transient traffic. An aerobatic aircraft in the middle of a loop is not looking for you.

If you do choose to transit one, treat it like a hornet's nest you have decided to walk past: know what the suffix letter says the activity is, monitor and announce on the published frequency where one exists, keep your eyes outside, and pick an altitude that stays away from the core of the activity if the zone's vertical limits give you room. Better yet, when the detour costs two minutes, take the detour.

How do I get permission for a CYR?

A CYR is different in kind, not just degree. The CARs prohibit entry without authorization from the user agency: the controlling or operating authority named for that zone in the Designated Airspace Handbook. For most CYRs that agency is military; for others it may be another government body or a designated operator. The DAH entry for the zone tells you who it is and, where applicable, how to make contact.

The process is exactly what it sounds like: identify the user agency from the DAH, contact them before the flight, and get an explicit authorization for your transit. No response is a no. If the zone is one of the CYRs that activates by NOTAM and it is cold during your window, the restriction may not be in force, but confirm that from the NOTAM and the DAH entry rather than assuming: some CYRs are continuously active, and a chart glance does not distinguish them.

It is worth saying plainly: an inflight "it looked inactive" is not a defense. If you cannot establish that a CYR is inactive or that you have permission, plan around it.

What is a CYD, and why "danger" instead of "restricted"?

A danger area is what a restricted area becomes when it sits over international waters. Canada cannot legally restrict foreign aircraft in international airspace, so instead of prohibiting entry, the CYD designation marks the hazard and leaves the decision to you. Entering a CYD is legal. That is the whole difference: the naval gunnery exercise inside it is no less real than it would be inside a CYR. Treat an active CYD exactly as you would treat a restricted area: stay out unless you have solid information that the activity is not running.

How do I know if a zone is active?

Class F zones come in three activation flavours: continuously active, active on a published schedule, or activated by NOTAM. The chart shows you the boundary; it does not tell you which flavour you are looking at or whether the zone is hot right now. For that:

  • The Designated Airspace Handbook (DAH) is the authoritative source for every zone's dimensions, altitudes, activity schedule, and user agency. NAV CANADA publishes it on a 56-day cycle, so the current issue is never far from the current reality.
  • The Canada Flight Supplement and VFR charts carry the zones too, and are the practical in-cockpit reference, but always confirm against the current cycle when a zone matters to your route.
  • NOTAMs carry activations, deactivations, and schedule changes for NOTAM-activated zones. Check them for your route and window as part of every briefing; the Canadian NOTAM guide covers how to read them.
  • A Flight Information Centre (1-866-WX-BRIEF within Canada) can tell you the status of a specific zone when the paperwork leaves you unsure.

The failure mode to avoid is the stale database: a zone that was redrawn or rescheduled two DAH cycles ago, still rendered from old data in whatever app you are using. (FlightDecide's Canadian airspace layer is extracted from the current DAH cycle and refreshed automatically when NAV CANADA publishes a new issue, so route checks against CYA, CYR, and CYD zones do not rely on a stale database.) Whatever tool you fly with, know which DAH issue its airspace came from.

Putting it together on a real route

Say your VFR route skims a CYA 5(S) and passes 8 miles from a CYR. The preflight sequence is short: check the DAH or CFS entries for both zones' hours and altitudes; check NOTAMs for activations or changes during your window (this folds into the same briefing as your weather check); decide whether the soaring area is likely active on a sunny Saturday afternoon (it is) and route around or above it; confirm the CYR's lateral limits and give it an honest margin rather than shaving the boundary. Ten minutes of paperwork, and Class F goes from a purple surprise to a known quantity, one more input alongside the rest of your NOTAM triage.
