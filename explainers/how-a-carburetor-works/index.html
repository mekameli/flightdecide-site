How a carburetor works

Home › Explainers › How a carburetor works

The short version

An older Cessna 172, like the 172P, has a carburetor under its engine. Gravity feeds it fuel, and a difference in air pressure pushes that fuel into the airflow.

You manage it with three knobs: the throttle, the red mixture knob and carb heat, which clears ice that can form inside it even on a hot day.

How the carburetor meters fuel

As the pistons draw air in, outside air comes through a filter and up into the bottom of the 172P's up-draft carburetor. On the way it squeezes through a narrow throat called the venturi. It speeds up to get through, and its pressure drops.

Beside the throat sits the float bowl, where a float holds the fuel at a steady level. The bowl is vented, so the air above its fuel stays at a higher pressure than the air in the throat. That difference pushes fuel up a passage and out of the discharge nozzle into the airstream, where it breaks up and evaporates.

Above the nozzle, the throttle knob turns a flat butterfly plate. Push the knob in and the plate turns toward edge-on. More air flows, the pressure drop grows, and more fuel comes with it.

Why you lean the mixture up high

The engine burns fuel by the weight of air it gets, but the venturi meters the volume of air. As you climb, each cubic foot of air weighs less, yet it still draws about the same fuel. The mixture turns rich, too much fuel for the air, and the engine can run rough.

Pulling the red mixture knob out turns a valve in the carburetor that cuts the fuel back, which leans the mixture. The 172P's handbook keeps it rich in the climb below 3,000 feet and leans for best RPM above that. In cruise it leans past peak RPM until the RPM drops 25 to 50. Pulled full out to idle cut-off, the knob stops the fuel to shut the engine down.

Coming down from altitude, push it back in. Too lean a mixture can run rough and overheat the engine.

Why it can ice on a hot day

The venturi's pressure drop chills the air, and so does the fuel evaporating into it. Together they can chill it by as much as 70 Fahrenheit degrees. If the air is humid, its water vapor can freeze onto the throat and the throttle plate, even on a 100 °F day.

The risk is worst with the throttle nearly closed in a descent. The air gets past the plate only through a small gap at its edge, and a little ice can block it.

The 172P's propeller is fixed-pitch, so lost power shows as lower RPM. The first sign is a drop in RPM with the throttle where you left it, then roughness. Pull the carb heat knob all the way out. It swaps the filtered air for unfiltered air warmed by a shroud around an exhaust pipe. The 172P's emergency checklist adds full throttle, then turns the heat off once the engine runs smoothly.

Three mistakes with carb ice

Keep full carb heat on when the RPM dips. Warm air is less dense, so the RPM drops first, then rises slowly as the ice melts.

Put full carb heat on before you close the throttle when icing is possible, without waiting for a symptom. A throttled-back engine cools quickly, which makes icing more likely. The 172P's handbook does so before reducing power to land.

Expect carb ice well above freezing. Carb ice is most likely with outside air below about 70 °F and humidity above 80 percent. Other airplanes differ, so follow the handbook for yours.

Check yourself

1. What pushes the fuel into the venturi?

2. Why does the mixture get richer as you climb?

3. In cruise, the RPM slowly drops though you haven't touched the throttle. What do you do, and what will the RPM do next?

4. Why put carb heat on before you close the throttle for a descent?

Answers

1. The higher air pressure in the vented float bowl, against the lower pressure in the venturi's throat.

2. The venturi meters air by volume, so thinner air draws about the same fuel but brings less weight of air.

3. Pull full carb heat. The RPM dips first, because warm air is less dense, then rises as the ice melts.

4. A little ice can block the small gap at the nearly closed plate, and a throttled-back engine cools, inviting ice.
