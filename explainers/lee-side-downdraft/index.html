Lee-side downdraft

The air can sink faster than you can climb.

Home › Explainers › Lee-side downdraft

air spilling down the downwind side of a ridge can descend faster than a light aircraft can climb, even at a climb attitude

What the wind does at a ridge

Picture yourself flying toward a ridge with the wind at your back. The air ahead of you reaches the ridge first. It has to go somewhere, and most often it goes over or around.

The side of the ridge facing into the wind is the windward side. The side facing away is the lee side. That is the side you arrive on after you cross.

What the air does next depends on how stable it is. Stable air is air that resists being pushed up or down. When stable air meets a ridge in a strong enough wind, a wave forms.

Unstable air behaves differently. It tends to keep rising and, given enough moisture, builds deep clouds or thunderstorms. This article is about the stable case, where the lifted air comes back down.

Why the air sinks behind the ridge

Stable air that has been lifted ends up heavier than the air around it at its new height. Gravity pulls it back toward the level it started from. Once it clears the crest, nothing holds it up.

So it drops down the lee side, and it gathers speed as it goes. The handbook describes the air accelerating as it passes over the crest and down the lee slopes.

The fall does not stop neatly at the old level. Momentum carries the air past it. Squeezed by the higher pressure lower down, it warms and becomes lighter than its surroundings.

Now it rises again, overshoots again, and keeps swinging up and down until the motion dies out. That swing is a gravity wave, named for the force that keeps pulling the air back.

The first downward leg of that swing sits right behind the ridge. That is exactly where you are in the minutes after a crossing.

How fast the sinking air can go

Your climb rate in a light aircraft has a ceiling. The weight, the air density and the power you have set it. The sinking air on the lee side does not care what that number is.

The handbook's turbulence chapter lists downdrafts faster than an aircraft can climb as a real concern near mountains. It says downdrafts greater than 1,500 fpm are possible, with localized gusts over 50 kt.

Find your own best rate of climb in your POH, the pilot's operating handbook for your airplane. Put it next to 1,500 fpm. If the air sinks faster than you climb through it, you lose height.

The handbook does not give one downdraft speed for every ridge. How strongly the wave moves the air depends on the wind across the ridge, the stability of the air, and the height and shape of the terrain.

Nose up and still losing height

Now put yourself in the cockpit on the lee side. Climb power is set and the nose sits at your usual climb attitude. Out the windshield it looks like every climb you have flown.

That attitude sets how fast you climb through the air around you. It tells you nothing about what that air is doing. If the whole mass of air is sinking, you are climbing inside something that is going down.

Your height over the ground is the two added together. Your climb through the air, minus the air's own descent, can come out below zero.

When it does, you are pitched up, at climb power, and losing height. The picture outside stays steady the whole time. Nothing in the attitude changes to warn you.

The instruments that show the sink

Pitch and power are what you put in. The altimeter shows what you actually get. So does the vertical speed indicator, the gauge that shows your rate of climb or descent.

In calm air the inputs and the outputs agree. Because they usually agree, it is easy to treat a climb attitude as proof of a climb.

Behind a ridge they can disagree. The attitude says climb while the altimeter unwinds and the vertical speed needle points down.

When that happens, the altimeter is the one telling you where you are. The attitude only tells you what you asked the airplane to do.

Where else the sinking air turns up

The sink is not limited to the slope right behind the crest. Some waves carry on far downwind as a train of rises and falls. These are called trapped lee waves, because their energy stays below a certain height.

They often show up as lens-shaped clouds lined up in bands parallel to the ridge, downstream from it. They also often form with no cloud at all. The handbook flags them for takeoff, landing and en route flight below flight level 250.

The size of the ridge is not a safe guide either. The handbook notes that even modest terrain can produce real wave activity. Its own simulation of trapped lee waves sits behind a 300-m-high mountain.

Other waves travel upward instead of downwind. Their effects can reach far above the peaks, at times beyond 60,000 ft. Aircraft at almost any height can meet the altitude swings they cause.

What this teaches

  • On the lee side of a ridge the air can be descending, and it can descend faster than a light aircraft climbs.
  • So a climb attitude does not guarantee a climb: the aircraft can be pitched up, at climb power, and still losing height.
