How the stall horn works

Home › Explainers › How the stall horn works

The short version

The 172P's stall horn is pneumatic, and air drawn through it makes the sound. It reacts to angle of attack, the angle at which the wing meets the oncoming air, and it doesn't measure airspeed.

That is why it sounds 5 to 10 knots above the stall in all flight conditions, steep turns included. Test it on every walkaround with suction through a clean handkerchief over its opening.

Where the inlet and the horn sit

Walk to the left wing and look at its leading edge, the rounded front edge that meets the air first. The small opening there is the stall warning inlet.

Plumbing runs from that inlet to an air-operated horn near the upper left corner of the windshield. The wing end senses the stall coming, and the horn makes the sound.

What the air does at the inlet

At a low angle of attack the air meets the wing at a stagnation point. That is a spot close to the leading edge where the flow virtually stops. The inlet sits there, so the air just pushes in and the horn stays quiet.

Raise the nose and slow down. As the angle grows, the point where the air splits slides lower, under the leading edge. The low pressure on the upper surface moves forward around the leading edge, over the inlet.

The inlet now sits in lower pressure than the cabin air at the horn. That difference pulls cabin air in through the horn, along the tube and out of the slot. The reed sounds.

Why it still warns in a steep turn

For a given flap setting the 172P always stalls at the same angle of attack. Weight and bank angle don't change that angle.

The stall speed does change. Roll into a level 60-degree bank and the load factor, the load on the wing compared with straight-and-level flight, becomes 2. To hold altitude you add angle of attack, so the wing reaches its stall angle at a higher speed.

A load factor of 2 multiplies the stall speed by the square root of 2, about 1.41, a 41 percent rise. At 2,400 lb with flaps up and power off, that takes the stall from about 44 KIAS, knots of indicated airspeed, to about 62 KIAS.

The horn doesn't read airspeed. It answers the low pressure at the inlet, which follows the angle.

Three mistakes the horn invites

Check the stall warning opening for stoppage on every walkaround. It is item 3 of the left wing's leading edge check. Put a clean handkerchief over it before you apply suction, and a sound from the horn confirms the system works.

Ease the nose down when the horn sounds while you maneuver, whatever the airspeed indicator shows. In a steep turn it can sound at a speed that would feel comfortable wings level. Less angle of attack silences it. Then roll the wings level and add power. The POH notes that a stall recovery may lose as much as 230 feet.

Keep reading the wing's own cues when the horn stays quiet. A stall warning system can fail. Aerodynamic buffet, loss of roll authority or a descent you cannot stop still point to a stall.

Check yourself

1. In a 172P at 2,400 lb, flaps up and power off, roughly what is the stall speed in a level 60-degree bank?

2. Why does a horn that doesn't read airspeed still sound before the stall in that steep turn?

3. You apply suction through a handkerchief over the stall warning opening and hear nothing. What has the check told you?

4. Which cue do you trust when the airframe buffets and the ailerons lose authority but the horn stays quiet?

Answers

1. About 62 KIAS. A load factor of 2 multiplies the 44 KIAS wings-level stall by the square root of 2, about 1.41. The horn sounds 5 to 10 knots above that.

2. It reacts to the low pressure reaching the inlet, which follows angle of attack. The wing stalls at the same angle in the turn, only at a higher speed.

3. Nothing proves it works yet. Check the opening for stoppage, seal the handkerchief and try again. If it stays silent, the stall warning isn't working, so have it fixed before you fly.

4. Trust the buffet and the weak ailerons. A stall warning system can fail, and those cues still tell you a stall is close, so lower the angle of attack.
