Acceleration that feels like a climb

With nothing to see outside, speeding up feels like pitching up.

Home › Explainers › Acceleration that feels like a climb

On a dark night takeoff, acceleration pushes the inner ear the same way a nose-up pitch would, and the instinct is to push down.

What a dark takeoff feels like

You line up at night at a coastal airfield. Past the runway lights there is water, and past the water there is nothing lit at all. You push the throttle up and the aeroplane begins to accelerate.

A few seconds later your body reports that the nose has risen, and risen more than you set it. Everything you feel says steep climb. The flight instruments say otherwise.

The FAA handbook lists this one under climbing while accelerating. Gaining speed, on its own, can feel like pitching the nose up.

Why speeding up feels nose-up

Start with what acceleration does to your body. As the aeroplane gains speed you are pressed back into the seat, and your back feels that push.

Now take the same seat and tilt the aeroplane nose-up. You are pressed back again, in much the same direction and much the same way. Your inner ear, the balance sense that tells you which way is up, gets a very similar push from both.

In daylight the argument ends at once, because you look outside and the horizon settles it. On a black night over water there is no horizon to look at. Nothing contradicts the sensation, so the sensation stands.

Why the nose ends up down

A pilot who feels a steep climb does the reasonable thing about it. You ease the nose down to bring it back. Since the climb was never there, what you have really done is point the aeroplane lower.

That correction is worst in the first minute after takeoff. You are low, the surface below is unlit, and there is nothing outside to show the nose going down.

The handbook's own summary of these demonstrations is blunt about the body. Its read on the aeroplane's attitude is wrong often enough that you cannot use it.

Meeting it with an instructor

You can meet this illusion on purpose, at height, with an instructor flying and looking outside. The handbook sets out the exercise. You close your eyes while the aeroplane is held straight and level at approach airspeed, the speed you would fly on final.

Then the instructor accelerates and holds that same straight and level attitude. With your eyes shut, what you usually feel is a climb. Opening them shows an aeroplane whose attitude has not changed at all.

Do not fly this at low altitude, and not without an instructor pilot or a safety pilot. There are also ground devices that produce these illusions with no aeroplane involved, such as a Barany chair or a Vertigon.

Flying pitch on the instruments

With no horizon outside, your pitch comes off the flight instruments and nowhere else. That means reading them and acting on them while your body insists on something different. The instruction in the handbook is to trust the instruments and set the sensation aside.

The sensation itself does not go away. It is a normal thing for a pilot to feel, and training does not delete it. What training builds is the habit of flying the instruments anyway, and pilots who stay proficient on instruments are less affected.

So the work is done before the night takeoff, not during it. Get training and keep your proficiency in aircraft control by reference to the instruments before you fly where no horizon is evident. Over open water at night counts, and so does visibility less than 3 miles.

What this teaches

  • Forward acceleration can produce a false sensation of a nose-up pitch attitude.
  • Without outside visual references the pilot may lower the nose toward the surface, so pitch must be flown on the flight instruments rather than on what the body reports.
