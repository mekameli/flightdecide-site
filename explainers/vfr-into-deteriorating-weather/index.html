VFR into deteriorating weather

Your room to turn closes from above and below.

Home › Explainers › VFR into deteriorating weather

A descending cloud base and rising terrain close the same gap from both sides, so the room left shrinks faster than either alone.

What the picture shows

The labels on the illustration, and what each one is pointing at.

  • LESS SKY EACH MILEThe tapering wedge of clear air midway along the valley, between the cloud underside and the rising slope.
  • NO ROOM TO TURNThe pinch ahead of the aircraft where the cloud underside settles onto the third ridge.
  • THIS TURN STILL FITSThe ghosted 180-degree turn arc around the ghosted aircraft, back in the wide part of the band.

Why the gap shrinks fast

You are following a valley under a cloud deck that has been sinking all morning. The floor of the valley climbs ahead of you toward a pass. The clear air you are flying in has a top and a bottom, and both are moving toward each other.

A base that keeps lowering eats into that band from above. Ground that keeps rising eats into it from below. Working together they take the same room from two sides, so it closes sooner than either one alone would close it.

What matters here is not your height above sea level. It is the height between the ground beneath you and the cloud above you. That is the number coming down.

When the ridges vanish

Further up the valley, the ridge you were using as a landmark stops being there. Cloud is sitting on it, or rain is hanging in front of it. The rock has not moved and you can no longer see it.

Mountains and ridges hidden like that have a name, mountain obscuration. Cloud does it, and so does precipitation or smoke.

Flight over a mountain route becomes far more hazardous once the mountains are obscured. You are threading between high ground you cannot see, working from a picture you built before takeoff.

What staying underneath costs you

When the base comes down, the pull is to go down with it. Staying below the cloud keeps you VFR and keeps the ground in sight. It also puts you nearer the terrain that is rising to meet you.

Going lower to stay out of cloud raises the risk of flying into ground you can no longer see. The murk under a lowering base takes your forward visibility first. Grey rock behind grey rain reads as more valley.

You lose the useful view before the cloud meets the ridge. The squeeze arrives earlier than the picture out the window suggests.

Numbers to set before takeoff

How low is too low is a bad question to answer in the air. During planning, read the safe altitudes for your route off the VFR and IFR charts. Write them down leg by leg.

Maximum elevation figures are the quickest source for that. They are the large numbers printed inside each grid square on a sectional, covering the highest terrain or obstacle in that square. Used with other data you can get before you go, they cut the chance of an inflight collision with terrain or an obstacle.

In mountain country, set a higher ceiling and visibility minimum than you would accept over flat ground. Raise it again when the terrain is new to you. Ridges you have never flown give you nothing to check the view against.

Where your outs come from

The weather turns out different from the forecast often enough that the other version of the day deserves a plan. Work that out before you leave. Pick a second airport you would be happy to land at, and a route you would rather fly if the pass closes.

An alternative helps only if you are willing to use it. Be ready to divert the moment the view stops matching the plan you made. That call is much easier on the ground than in a narrowing valley.

Turning around costs you nothing while the band of clear air is still wide. Leave it until the cloud settles onto the ridge and there is no room left to turn in.

What this teaches

  • When cloud or precipitation hides the mountains and ridges ahead, that is mountain obscuration, and flight over mountain routes becomes especially hazardous.
  • The weather may turn out different than forecast, so have alternative plans and be ready and willing to divert if it changes.
  • To avoid terrain and obstacles in low visibility, determine safe altitudes in advance from the altitudes shown on VFR and IFR charts.
  • Use maximum elevation figures and other easily obtainable data to minimize the chance of an inflight collision with terrain or obstacles.
  • In mountainous terrain, consider higher minimums for ceiling and visibility, particularly if the terrain is unfamiliar.
  • Staying underneath the clouds to remain VFR raises the risk of flying into terrain you can no longer see.
