What is parasite drag?

Home › Explainers › What is parasite drag?

The short version

Parasite drag is all the drag that does not come from making lift. It does nothing to help the airplane fly, and it comes in three kinds: form drag, skin friction and interference drag.

Every blunt shape, rough patch and joint adds some, and all of it grows quickly with speed.

Form drag comes from shape

Form drag comes from an object's shape and the way the air flows around it.

A bare wheel is blunt.

Air piles up against its front at higher pressure, then tears away behind it into a churning, low-pressure wake. With more pressure pushing on the front than on the back, the wheel is pushed backward, and that push is form drag.

Form drag is the easiest kind for a designer to reduce, by streamlining. A teardrop cover around the wheel, called a wheel fairing or wheel pant, lets the air close in smoothly behind it, so the wake shrinks and so does the drag. On a Cessna 172R, the optional teardrop covers on the wheels are worth about 2 knots of cruise speed.

Skin friction and the boundary layer

Skin friction is the drag of moving air in contact with the airplane's surface.

The air touching the skin is almost still. Each layer above it moves a little faster, until the air moves at the speed of the air flowing around the airplane. That thin region is the boundary layer, and as its layers slide over each other they rub.

Near the leading edge the boundary layer flows smoothly, which is called laminar flow. Some way back it breaks down into swirls and eddies, which is turbulent flow, and turbulent flow makes more skin friction than laminar flow.

So designers use flush rivets, remove anything that sticks up above the wing surface and give the airplane a smooth, glossy finish.

Interference drag where parts meet

Interference drag comes from airstreams meeting, which sets up eddies and turbulence or blocks the smooth flow. The wing root makes a lot of it. There, air flowing around the fuselage collides with air flowing over the wing.

It is worst where two surfaces meet at a right angle, and the joined parts make more drag than the same parts would apart.

Smooth, curved fairings at the joints reduce it, and so does more distance between a lifting surface and anything hung near it.

Two mistakes that add drag

Clear every trace of frost off the wings, tail and other surfaces before takeoff, however thin. Frost, snow or ice on the leading edge or top of the wing, even a layer no thicker or rougher than coarse sandpaper, can cut lift by as much as 30 percent and add up to 40 percent more drag.

Keep the airplane clean and waxed. Dirt disrupts the smooth flow of air over the surface and adds drag.

What speed does to drag

All three kinds grow with the square of the airspeed. Double an airplane's airspeed, say from 60 to 120 knots, and its parasite drag becomes four times as great.

Induced drag, the drag that comes with making lift, does the opposite. In level flight at the same weight, at twice the speed it is about a quarter of what it was.

Check yourself

1. At the same altitude you fly twice as fast. How much bigger does the parasite drag get?

2. Why does a teardrop cover make less drag than a bare wheel?

3. Why do designers use flush rivets and a smooth, glossy finish?

4. A thin layer of frost as rough as coarse sandpaper covers the wing before a morning flight. What do you do?

Answers

1. About four times bigger, because parasite drag grows with the square of the airspeed.

2. The air closes in smoothly behind it, so the low-pressure wake shrinks and so does the form drag.

3. Anything that sticks up or roughens the skin adds skin friction drag.

4. Clean every bit of it off before the flight, because even that much frost adds drag and cuts lift.
