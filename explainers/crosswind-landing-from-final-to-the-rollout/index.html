Crosswind landing from final to the rollout

Home › Explainers › Crosswind landing from final to the rollout

The short version

In a crosswind, the yoke goes into the wind to stop the drift and the rudder keeps the nose straight. As you slow in the flare, the pitch-up that ends the descent, your controls do less, so you add more of both.

The landing isn't over at touchdown. Keep turning the yoke into the wind through the rollout, the slowdown on the runway, to full at the stop.

Crab or wing-low on final

Point the nose straight down the runway with no correction, and the wind drifts you sideways. One fix is a crab. Turn the nose into the wind until your track, your path over the ground, runs straight along the runway.

Touch down crabbed, though, and the wheels land at an angle and take a side load. So the crab comes out with rudder just before touchdown. Too early and you drift, too late and you land crabbed.

The wing-low method, recommended in most cases, holds the nose straight with rudder. You turn the yoke into the wind, which moves the ailerons, to lower the upwind wing just enough to stop the drift. The bank tries to turn you, so you hold opposite rudder.

The wing-low method is a sideslip, a bank held against opposite rudder. In a 172S, avoid steep slips with more than 20 degrees of flap, because the elevator can oscillate slightly. In a strong crosswind, use the least flap your runway allows.

Why you need more control as you slow

In the flare the airplane slows, and the controls get less effective. The correction you were holding is no longer enough, so add aileron and rudder gradually, all the way down.

Keep the upwind wing down through the flare, so the upwind main wheel touches first.

Drift at touchdown does real harm. If a tire's path runs just 10 degrees off the way it points, it takes a side load of half the weight on it. Enough of that can lift one main wheel and put a wingtip or the propeller on the runway.

Why the rollout still needs the yoke

As you slow, the headwind from your own motion shrinks. So the wind you feel swings toward the side and lifts the upwind wing harder. Less air over the ailerons makes the same yoke do less.

The airplane also tends to weathervane. The main wheels act as a pivot with more side area behind them than ahead. The wind pushes the tail around and turns the nose into it.

So keep adding aileron into the wind, holding it fully over as you stop. Steer with the pedals, which turn the nosewheel, and use a little brake if you need it.

Four crosswind mistakes to avoid

Hold the bank right to the ground. The low wingtip looks close but won't hit, and leveling the wings lets the airplane drift as it touches down.

Go around if full opposite rudder can't stop the turn. The wind is too strong for that runway, so land on one more into the wind. In a 172S go-around with more than 20 degrees of flap, reduce the flaps to 20 degrees right after full power.

Ease the rudder as the nosewheel touches. Rudder still held for the slip steers the nosewheel the moment it takes weight, and the airplane swerves.

Fly gusty approaches a little faster. The gust factor is how many knots the gusts run above the steady wind. Unless your POH says otherwise, add half of it to your normal approach speed.

Check yourself

1. With the wind from the right on short final, which wing do you lower?

2. Why does your crosswind correction run short in the flare, and what do you do about it?

3. What does it tell you when full opposite rudder can't stop the turn your bank creates?

4. With a 65-knot normal approach and the wind at 12 knots gusting 20, what speed do you fly?

Answers

1. The right wing, just enough to stop the drift, with left rudder to keep the nose straight.

2. The airplane slows in the flare, so the controls do less, and you add aileron and rudder as needed to stop the drift.

3. The wind is too strong for that runway, so go around and land on one more into the wind.

4. About 69 knots, since half the 8-knot gust factor added to 65 gives 69.
