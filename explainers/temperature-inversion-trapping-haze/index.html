A temperature inversion trapping haze

The inversion that smooths the air also traps the haze below.

Home › Explainers › A temperature inversion trapping haze

Smooth air and poor visibility arrive together, because the inversion that stops the mixing also traps what is in the air.

  • SMOOTH AIR INSIDE THE LAYERThe small profile aircraft flying wings-dead-level at the top of the haze, on the inversion line.

  • VISIBILITY LOST BELOW THE LIDThe far end of the valley below the line, where the ridges dissolve into grey with no edges left.

What a temperature inversion is

Air normally gets colder as you climb away from the ground. An inversion is a layer where that order turns over, with warmer air lying above cooler air.

Forecasters judge a layer by what happens to a small parcel of air pushed up into it. Inside an inversion, that parcel arrives colder and denser than the air around it. Being heavier, it settles back to the level it came from.

A profile like that is classed as absolute stability. Every parcel you could test in the layer resists being lifted, whether it is saturated or not.

Why the air stops mixing

Stability is a property of the air you are flying through, and it decides whether vertical motion grows or dies. A stable layer answers every upward nudge with a push back down. Motion that starts inside it does not get far.

Vertical motion is how low air and high air trade places. With that exchange shut off, the air under the layer stays under the layer.

The ride smooths out for the same reason. Bumps in the low levels are parcels rising and sinking, and a stable layer does not allow them to.

Why the haze stays below

Haze and smoke go where the air goes. Carried upward, they spread through a deep column and thin out. Held down, they stay in the shallow slice of air near the surface.

Under an inversion the second case applies. Whatever was already in the low air remains there, and anything added to it joins the same shallow layer. Concentration climbs because the volume available to it has stopped growing.

That slice is the air you look through in the pattern and on a low cross country. How far you can see is set by how much has gathered in it, not by how clear the sky is overhead.

What the smooth ride reports

Comfort in the cockpit is a readout of how little the air is moving up and down. It carries no information about what that same stillness has collected underneath you.

Both conditions come from one property of one layer. One layer damped the turbulence and stopped the ventilation at the same time.

Plan for the visibility to be poorest on the mornings the air feels best. A glassy climb out of a valley is a reason to check the reported visibility again, not a reason to relax about it.

When the trap lets go

Stability follows how fast temperature falls with height. Steepen that fall and stability drops.

Sun on the ground warms the air in contact with it, which steepens the profile from below. Once the heating has worked up through the layer, vertical motion returns. The load of haze then has a much deeper column to spread through.

Heating is not the only lever. Most changes in stability come from air moving in from somewhere else, so a layer can be built or broken by what arrives aloft.

What this teaches

  • A temperature inversion is a stable layer that suppresses vertical mixing.
  • Because mixing is suppressed, haze and smoke concentrate below the inversion while the air within it is smooth.
