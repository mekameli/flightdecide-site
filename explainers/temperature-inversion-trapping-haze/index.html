A temperature inversion trapping haze

The inversion that smooths the air also traps the haze below.

Home › Explainers › A temperature inversion trapping haze

Smooth air and poor visibility arrive together, because the inversion that stops the mixing also traps what is in the air.

Why the air goes smooth and milky

You level off on a warm, still morning and the ride goes glassy. The wings barely move. At the same time the horizon has turned soft and the ground ahead looks washed out.

One of those feels like good news and the other feels like bad news. They come from the same layer of air. That layer is a temperature inversion, a band where the air gets warmer as you climb instead of colder.

Making sense of it takes one idea from the handbook's chapter on stability. Stability is how strongly the air resists being moved up or down.

How air decides whether to rise

Picture a small bubble of air, which forecasters call a parcel, pushed up from the surface. As it rises it expands and cools. What it does next depends on the air around it.

If the lifted parcel ends up colder than its surroundings, it is denser and sinks back to where it started. That air is stable, because it resists being lifted. If the parcel ends up the same temperature, it simply stays put, which is called neutral.

If it ends up warmer and lighter, it keeps rising on its own until it matches the air around it. That air is unstable. The bigger the temperature difference, the faster the parcel moves.

So the way temperature changes with height decides how much up and down motion you get. That change is the lapse rate, the rate at which temperature falls as you climb.

Why an inversion is so stable

In most air the temperature drops as you go up. In an inversion it does the opposite. Warmer air sits on top of cooler air.

Run the parcel test inside that layer. A parcel pushed upward cools as it rises, while the air around it gets warmer with height. It is soon colder and heavier than its neighbors, so it sinks back.

The handbook puts inversions under absolute stability. That means a lapse rate smaller than the moist adiabatic lapse rate, the lesser rate at which saturated rising air cools. Layers where temperature stays the same with height, called isothermal, count too.

Its stability chapter closes on the point that matters here. Stable air holds vertical motion down, and unstable air speeds it up.

Why the haze stays trapped underneath

Smoke is fine particles in the air from fires, industrial burning and other sources. For visibility to improve near the ground, moving air has to spread those particles out.

Vertical mixing, air rising and sinking through the low layers, does much of that work. It carries particles upward and spreads them through a deeper layer. Less is then left in the air near the ground.

An inversion shuts that down. Air rising from below meets the stable layer and is pushed back, so the particles it carries stop at the same height.

They collect in the air beneath with nowhere higher to go. Whatever is being added near the ground keeps building up under the lid. The same particles are packed into a shallower slice of air, so the view through it gets worse.

What the smooth ride is telling you

The stable layer resists vertical motion of every kind. That same resistance is why the air within the inversion is smooth to fly through. The calm and the murk have one cause.

So treat a glassy ride as information about the air. It tells you vertical motion is being held down. When vertical motion is held down, whatever is in the low air stays there.

A milky horizon at the same moment fits the picture. The two together point to a stable layer, and to haze concentrated underneath it.

Keep the smooth air and the poor visibility in the same thought. Enjoying one while ignoring the other means reading only half of what the layer is telling you.

How daytime heating can lift the lid

Stability is not fixed through the day. Sun heating the ground makes temperature fall faster with height, which lowers stability. Cooling of the ground at night does the reverse and raises it.

With clear skies above a surface layer of haze or smoke, visibility generally improves during the day. The heating drives convective mixing, rising currents that carry the particles higher. Spread through a deeper layer, they thin out near the ground.

That improvement comes slowly, because the particles have to be carried off by moving air. A thick layer of cloud above the haze blocks the sunlight that drives the mixing. Visibility then improves little, if at all, through the day.

How big the day and night swing gets depends on the setting. The handbook lists what makes the temperature and stability change large or small.

What a new inversion does to dust

Dust in the air shows the same stability from the other side. Strong cooling after sunset quickly steadies the lowest air, forms an inversion, and lets the dust settle.

With no turbulence to hold it up, dust generally settles at 1,000 ft (300 m) per hour. Settling completely takes many hours, or even days. Rain and other precipitation remove dust far more effectively.

Looking down through it is harder than looking across. Slant range visibility, your line of sight angled down to the ground, is generally worse in dust than reported surface visibility. You may not be able to pick out an airfield from above even with 3 mi or more reported.

The settling happens because the stable air has taken away the stirring that held the dust up. It is the same stability that traps haze, doing a different job.

What this teaches

  • A temperature inversion is a stable layer that suppresses vertical mixing.
  • Because mixing is suppressed, haze and smoke concentrate below the inversion while the air within it is smooth.
