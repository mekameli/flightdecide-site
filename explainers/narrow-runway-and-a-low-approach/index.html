A narrow runway and a low approach

A narrow runway hides how low you really are.

Home › Explainers › A narrow runway and a low approach

A narrower runway than you are used to makes you feel higher than you are, and the uncorrected result is an approach that is too low.

What the picture shows

The labels on the illustration, and what each one is pointing at.

  • LOWER THAN IT FEELSThe solid aeroplane on the lower, shallower approach path.
  • NOT THE WIDTH YOU KNOWThe gap between the real runway edge and the pale outline of the usual width drawn around it.
  • NORMAL PATH, UP HEREThe ghost aeroplane on the upper, normal approach path.
  • TREES IN YOUR PATHThe conifer tops short of the threshold that the solid aeroplane's path grazes.

Why a narrow runway looks higher

Most of your landings happen at one home field, so its runway turns into your ruler. On final you judge your height partly from the runway's shape and size, and you learned that shape at home.

Seen from the air, a runway looks like a trapezoid, a shape wide at your end that narrows toward the far end. On a steady approach at a constant angle, that shape only grows. It doesn't change.

The shape changes when your angle changes. If you get shallow, the runway looks shorter and wider. If you get steep, it looks longer and narrower. That long, thin picture is the one you've learned to read as high.

Now fly to a runway narrower than the one you're used to. It hands you a thin picture from a normal height. The FAA's answer is that a narrower-than-usual runway can make you feel higher than you really are.

How the wrong picture becomes a low approach

You fly the picture you see. If it tells you you're high, you take off power and lower the nose to come down to where you think you should be.

That correction is the mistake. A pilot who doesn't spot the illusion flies a lower approach than normal, the one the card above shows under the ghost aeroplane.

A low path brings two risks. You can hit objects along the approach path, such as trees or buildings short of the threshold. Or you can land short, touching down before the runway starts.

The same trick works the other way round. A wider-than-usual runway can make you feel low, and the risk then is leveling out high and landing hard or running long. The AIM, at 8-1-5, ranks illusions among the most common factors cited in fatal aircraft accidents.

Spotting it before you reach the field

The best defence starts on the ground. Expect visual illusions when you fly to an airport you don't know, and expect them more at night or in bad weather.

Before you go, read the airport diagram and the Chart Supplement, the FAA directory of airport details. They tell you about runway slope, the terrain around it and the lighting.

Width is only one of the things the FAA lists that push an approach low. Several others lead to the same result, so brief them together for any new field.

If you can, look the airport over from the air before you land. And keep your landing skills sharp. The FAA names both as ways to head off these errors.

What to trust on final instead

Your eyes still do most of the work on a visual approach, but they need a check that doesn't care how wide the runway is. The FAA says to look at the altimeter often on every approach, day and night.

Use a VASI or PAPI whenever one is there. Both are sets of lights beside the runway that give you a visual reference for the glide path. An electronic glideslope does the same job on instruments.

The aiming point gives you another check. That's the spot on the ground you're descending toward, and on a steady path it seems to stay still. If it creeps up toward the horizon, the point you'll really reach is closer than you think.

Don't stare at one spot. Move your eyes from the aiming point to the horizon, to objects along the runway, to the ground well short of it, and back again. Drifting off the path is easier to catch that way.

Watch out for distraction too. The FAA warns that the chance of an approach accident rises when an emergency or another task pulls you away from your usual routine.

Getting back up when you find yourself low

Say the cross-check shows you're low and undershooting. Add power and raise the nose to level off, holding your airspeed as you do it.

When you meet the proper glide path again, reduce power and lower the nose to stay on it. The fix uses power and pitch together, never one on its own.

The tempting mistake is pulling back to stretch the glide with no added power. That raises the angle of attack, the angle between the wing and the oncoming air, and bleeds off airspeed. You risk a fast sink or a stall close to the ground.

Don't pull the flaps up to fix an undershoot either. It can cost you lift suddenly, raise your sink rate and leave the approach less stable than before.

If you're very low, you may not be able to settle into a stabilized approach, a steady glide at a constant angle, speed and setup toward a chosen point. The normal answer then is a go-around.

What this teaches

  • A runway narrower than usual creates the illusion that the aircraft is higher than it actually is.
  • A pilot who does not recognise the illusion flies a lower-than-normal approach, with the risk of striking objects along the approach path or landing short.
