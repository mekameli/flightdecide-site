A microburst on final

Home › Explainers › A microburst on final

The short version

A microburst on final gives you a headwind that raises your airspeed, then a downdraft, then a tailwind that takes it away. That early gain is the warning. Don't reduce power to fix it, and be ready to go around. The margin you would trim off is the margin you need moments later.

What a microburst does to the wind

A column of air drops out of a shower or thunderstorm and spreads outward in all directions when it reaches the ground. That is a microburst, the most severe kind of wind shear, meaning a sudden change in wind speed or direction over a small area.

Going in, the outflow, the air racing away from the center, meets you as a headwind. In the middle the air pushes you down, and coming out the outflow runs your way as a tailwind.

Vertical winds usually peak more than 500 ft above the ground. In the center of a strong microburst, the downdraft can exceed 3,000 fpm (feet per minute). How hard it hits you depends on your height and how near the center you pass.

Why the airspeed gain is a trap

A rising headwind lifts your indicated airspeed and performance. The nose tends to pitch up as the airplane works back to its trim speed, leaving you fast and high.

Correcting that with less power and a lower nose sets up what comes next. The tailwind arrives and takes away airspeed and performance, and it finds you nose low with the power back. Recovery from there is harder, and the airplane can stall or land short of the runway.

So treat a rapid or large airspeed gain near convective weather, meaning showers and storms, as a possible sign of a coming loss. It may be reason enough to break off the approach.

When the warning never comes

A microburst's outflow can be symmetric, the same on every side, but it is often asymmetric, stronger on some sides than others. Depending on your path, the gain going in may never appear, or may be much smaller than the loss coming out.

An intense rain shaft at the surface can mark a microburst. Sometimes the only visible clue is virga, rain streaks that never reach the ground, or a ring of blowing dust. If you've met or seen one, watch for another, since the same weather system can produce more than one.

Three easy mistakes in a microburst

Go around at the first sign of a microburst. Only 5 to 15 seconds may be available to recognize it and recover. At low altitude, recovery may be impossible.

Hold the nose at its normal pitch or higher as the airspeed falls, and accept the slower speed. The airplane's natural response to low airspeed is to pitch down, and letting it go there adds to the height you lose.

Fly a stabilized approach, holding one steady speed and path, so a bad trend stands out early. Feeding in power bit by bit may mask the first drop in airspeed.

Check yourself

1. What should a sudden jump well above your target airspeed on final near a shower tell you?

2. Can you rule out a microburst near the airport if you see virga below the cloud base but no rain at the surface?

3. How should you handle pitch when the airspeed starts falling partway down final and the nose wants to drop?

4. If you fly into a microburst's outflow and feel no airspeed gain at all, can you expect a gentle exit?

Answers

1. It may be a microburst's headwind, with a tailwind loss to follow. Keep the power in and be ready to go around.

2. No. Virga or a ring of blowing dust is sometimes the only visible sign of a microburst.

3. Hold pitch at normal or higher and accept the lower airspeed. Letting the nose drop to chase speed costs height you can't spare.

4. No. In an asymmetric microburst the gain going in may be small or missing, and the loss coming out much larger.
