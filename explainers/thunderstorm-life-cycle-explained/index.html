The thunderstorm life cycle, explained

Home › Explainers › The thunderstorm life cycle, explained

The short version

Every thunderstorm cell goes through three stages. As a towering cumulus, it grows on a strong updraft. It turns mature when its rain reaches the ground, and it dies once sinking air has taken over.

Keep at least 20 miles from any thunderstorm identified as severe or giving an intense radar echo. A good rule of thumb is 20 nautical miles, since hail may fall for miles outside the cloud. If you can't get around the storms, divert and wait them out on the ground.

What starts a cell and builds it

A storm needs enough water vapor, unstable air and a push to lift it. The push can be a front, wind up a slope, converging winds, a sea or lake breeze, or cool air spreading from an older storm. Storms that pop up at random in unstable air are generally a result of surface heating.

Pushed hard enough, unstable air keeps rising until it is no warmer than the air around it. If it cools to saturation on the way, a cumulus cloud forms.

In the towering cumulus stage, the updraft is a warm bubble near the cloud's top that can exceed 3,000 feet per minute (fpm). It is strong enough to hold the rain up.

How a cell matures, dies and is replaced

Within about 15 minutes, the drops grow too heavy for the updraft to hold, and their fall brings on the mature stage. The rain drags air down with it, making a downdraft beside the updraft. The strongest turbulence in the cloud lies between the two.

At the ground, the downdraft spreads well ahead of the cell as cool, gusty air. Its curved leading edge is the gust front. Up high, strong winds flatten the top into an anvil, and the hazards peak late in this stage.

In the dissipating stage, sinking air fills the cloud and cuts off its moisture supply. The downdraft stays strong in the rain as the rain tapers off. Warmed as it sinks, the air dries the cloud from below until only the old anvil is left.

From first tower to old anvil typically takes about 30 minutes, but single-cell storms are rare. In an organized cluster, new cells form upwind as old ones drift downwind, or rise on the lift along a gust front. Such a cluster can last several hours.

What a mature storm throws at you

Not every storm holds every hazard in the table, and none shows from outside which it holds. Over the top is no escape in a light airplane: severe storms can reach 50,000 to 60,000 feet.

Icing builds fast because the updraft carries water that is still liquid above the freezing level, and it freezes on impact. Caught inside, keep the power set for the manual's turbulence penetration airspeed and hold a constant attitude. Let altitude and airspeed vary.

Four mistakes near a thunderstorm

Stay out from under a storm and its anvil, even when you can see daylight beyond. Turbulence and wind shear make the air under the storm hazardous. Under the anvil, severe and extreme turbulence can strike in clear air.

Don't take off or land in front of an approaching storm. Its gust front arrives first, and a sudden burst of low-level turbulence can cost you control.

Plan your route around storms with the datalink radar picture, never using it alone to thread between cells. It can lag the age it shows by 15 to 20 minutes. Never fly between severe or intense echoes less than 40 miles apart.

Treat every thunderstorm as dangerous, even when its radar echo is light. A lone cell can still be severe, with hail and microburst winds.

Check yourself

1. A cumulus ahead has a hard, growing top and no rain under its base. Which stage is it in, and what holds the rain up?

2. You're holding short when the windsock swings and a cool, gusty wind arrives, with the rain still miles off. What reached you, and should you take off?

3. The cell you've watched has stopped raining and is breaking up, while new towers build beside it. Is the area about to clear?

Answers

1. The towering cumulus stage. Its strong updraft keeps the drops from falling.

2. A gust front, and no. Wait on the ground until the storm passes, because its turbulence can cost you control.

3. No. It's a multicell storm, and those new towers are new cells that will mature in turn.
