How low pressure and cold air fool your altimeter

Home › Explainers › How low pressure and cold air fool your altimeter

The short version

Your altimeter turns air pressure into feet. Fly from high pressure into a low, an area of lower pressure, without resetting it, and the same reading puts you lower. Cold air does the same, and no setting fixes it.

Reset to the setting from a nearby airport's weather station as you go. In cold air over high ground, plan a higher altitude.

Why a low takes altitude from you

An altimeter is a barometer. It measures the air pressure around the airplane and shows it as altitude, counted from the pressure you set in its window.

Hold a steady reading, and you fly along one pressure level, a height where the air pressure has one fixed value. Fly into a low without resetting, and that level sits lower, so you sink with it.

Settings are in inches of mercury, such as 29.92, and each inch is worth about 1,000 feet. Depart in a Cessna 172 with 30.12 set, cruise at 6,500 feet and fly on to where the setting is 29.62. That half inch leaves you about 500 feet lower than you read.

Below 18,000 feet, get the current setting from a station along your route, within 100 nautical miles, and dial it in. With 29.62 dialed in, the same airplane reads 6,000, so climb back to 6,500. That removes the pressure error, though not the cold-air error in the next section.

Why cold air puts you lower still

Setting the pressure does nothing for temperature. Standard air is 15 °C at sea level and cools about 2 °C per 1,000 feet. Colder air is denser, so its pressure levels sit closer together and the column of air below you is shorter.

Your altimeter still assumes standard spacing, so it shows more height above the station than is really there. The squeeze adds up with height, so the error grows the higher you are above the station and the colder it is there.

What it adds up to over a ridge

Take the 172 at 6,500 feet on the altimeter over a 5,500-foot ridge, near a reporting station at 3,000 feet. Yesterday that left 1,000 feet. Tonight the setting there is 29.62, you still have 30.12 set, and it is minus 20 °C at the station.

With the right setting you would read 6,000, which is 3,000 feet above the station, so the table gives up to 420 feet. Worst case for these two errors, that leaves as little as 80 feet over the crest.

Three altimeter mistakes to avoid

  • Check the altimeter on the ground. With the local setting in, it should read within 75 feet of where you stand, usually the field elevation. That is the airport's published elevation above sea level. If it is off by 75 feet or more, have a certificated instrument repair station check it.

  • Keep resetting as you fly. Air traffic control tells you when there is a new setting, and on your own you can hear one on a nearby airport's weather broadcast.

  • Climb early when it's cold over high ground on a flight under visual flight rules. There is no knob for cold, so add the cold error for your height above the station to the altitude you need. Take it from the full cold temperature error table in the Aeronautical Information Manual, or work it out on a flight computer. Climb to that altitude before the ridge, or turn back.

Check yourself

  4. Flying from a low into a high without resetting, which way is your altimeter wrong?
