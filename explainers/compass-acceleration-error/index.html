Compass acceleration error

Speed up heading east and the compass shows a turn toward north.

Home › Explainers › Compass acceleration error

The short version

On an east or west heading, the compass shows a turn toward north as you gain speed and toward south as you lose it. Pilots carry it as ANDS, Acceleration North, Deceleration South.

That swing is an error, so keep the wings level and take a fresh reading once the airspeed stops changing.

How the compass card hangs

Inside the compass, two small magnets ride on a metal float, sealed in a bowl of clear fluid much like kerosene. The card is a scale wrapped around the float. You read it through a window against the lubber line, a fixed mark across the glass.

Its letters mark north, east, south and west, with a number every 30° between them and the last zero left off. So 3 means 30°, 6 means 60° and 33 means 330°.

The float hangs from a pivot with its center of gravity below it, like a pendulum. Earth's magnetic field runs level with the ground only at the Magnetic Equator, halfway between the magnetic poles. Everywhere else it tilts, and that tilt against the ground is called magnetic dip.

Because the float hangs low and is heavy, the field's downward pull is too weak to tip it, so the card stays level. This hanging, or pendulous, mounting is what lets one compass work at all latitudes away from the poles without a correction for dip.

Why a speed change tips the card

You're eastbound, and you push the power up. The float has inertia, the tendency of a mass to resist a change in its motion, so it lags behind as the airplane gathers speed. That lag tips the aft end of the card up, so its front tips down. Slowing down tips it the other way.

Now the card is no longer level, and the compass reads correctly only while it is. On the tilted card, the field's downward pull drags the magnet's north end down, and that turns the card so the reading swings toward north.

That rotation of the card is acceleration error. The reading moves although your heading hasn't, and it stops once a steady speed lets the card hang level again.

Everything here is the Northern Hemisphere case. There the field dips downward toward the north, and the dip grows steeper as you near the Magnetic North Pole.

Which headings show the error

Point the nose north or south and a speed change brings no compass error. Climbing and diving on those headings bring none either.

East and west are where it shows. Eastbound, a false turn toward north makes the heading numbers fall, and westbound it makes them rise. Slowing down reverses both.

Three easy mistakes with a power change

Hold the wings level and let the card swing. Turning to chase the reading back steers you off a heading you never left.

Set the heading indicator, the gyro instrument you line up with the compass, only in steady flight. Set it during a speed change and it copies the error. If the card still wobbles once you're steady, use the average of its swings.

Make power changes smoothly and read the compass only once the airspeed has settled, not when the throttle stops moving. Abrupt changes of speed or attitude send the card moving erratically and make it very hard to read.

Check yourself

1. What has your heading done if, flying east, the reading creeps from E toward 6 while the speed builds after you add power?

2. Are you turning if, heading west with the wings level, the reading slides from W toward 24 as you slow down?

3. Should the compass swing when you're flying north and the airspeed rises after you add power?

4. Should you set the heading indicator to the compass while flying east with the speed still building?

Answers

1. It hasn't changed. That swing is acceleration error, and 6 means 60°, on the north side of E.

2. No. 24 sits on the south side of W, which is exactly where slowing down drags the reading on a west heading.

3. No. North is one of the two headings where a change of airspeed brings no compass error.

4. No. Wait until the speed is steady, or you set the heading indicator to the false turn toward north.
