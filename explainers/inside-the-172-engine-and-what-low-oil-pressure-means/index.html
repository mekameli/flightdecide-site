Inside the 172 engine and what low oil pressure means

Home › Explainers › Inside the 172 engine and what low oil pressure means

The short version

If the oil pressure in your 172 drops below its green arc, the normal range, in flight, check the oil temperature. A normal temperature may point to the gauge, its line or the relief valve, but it doesn't prove the engine is fine. Land at the nearest airport.

If the pressure is gone and the temperature is climbing, the engine may fail soon. Reduce power now, pick a landing spot and use only the power you need to reach it.

How the engine turns the propeller

The 172P's Lycoming O-320 has four cylinders, laid flat in two opposed pairs and cooled by air. It is rated 160 horsepower at 2,700 revolutions per minute. Other 172 models have other engines and gauge markings, so check your own airplane's handbook.

In each cylinder, the burning fuel and air push a piston, a sliding plug, toward the crankshaft, the engine's main turning shaft. Its connecting rod pushes on an offset part of the crankshaft, which turns the push into rotation. The engine is direct drive, so the crankshaft turns the propeller itself.

Above the crankshaft runs the camshaft. Through hollow tubes called pushrods, it opens each cylinder's valves, which let the fuel and air in and the burned gas out.

Where the oil goes

Two of oil's jobs matter here. It lubricates the moving parts, and it carries heat away from the cylinders.

The engine-driven pump draws it from the sump, the oil reservoir at the bottom of the engine, through a screen. Cold oil goes straight to the filter, and hot oil goes through the cooler first.

A relief valve limits the pressure by spilling the extra oil back to the sump. The rest flows into the main oil gallery, the engine's main oil passage. From there it reaches the bearings, the smooth supports for the turning shafts, the camshaft and, through the hollow pushrods, the valves.

Then it drains back to the sump by gravity. So if the oil stops flowing, the parts rub, the heat stays in the engine and the temperature climbs.

What the two oil gauges tell you

The oil pressure gauge reads straight from the engine through a line. It shows pressure, not how much oil is left. The oil temperature gauge beside it is your cross-check.

With a normal temperature, a faulty gauge can read low falsely, or a faulty relief valve can make the pressure really low. A leak in the gauge line isn't necessarily a reason to land at once. An orifice, a narrow restriction in that line, lets so little oil through that a leak can't drain the sump quickly.

If the pressure is gone and the temperature is rising, an engine failure may be close.

Three mistakes with low oil pressure

  • Keep watching the temperature after a normal first reading. Oil temperature changes more slowly than pressure, so a normal reading soon after the drop doesn't clear the engine.

  • Reduce power at once if the temperature climbs with the pressure gone, and get ready to land without engine power.

  • Land at the nearest airport even when the gauge looks like the culprit. Treat any abnormal reading as a reason to cut the flight short.
