VMC and the engine-out roll in a twin

Home › Explainers › VMC and the engine-out roll in a twin

The short version

When one engine of a twin quits, the good engine swings the nose toward the dead one. Rudder on the good engine's side holds it straight, helped by a small bank toward that engine.

The rudder's grip fades as you slow. Below VMC, the minimum control speed, even full rudder can't hold the nose, and the swing turns into a roll toward the dead engine. If the swing won't stop, reduce power on the good engine and lower the nose.

Why one dead engine swings the nose

Say the left engine quits in the climb. The right engine still pulls on its side of the airplane, so the nose yaws, or swings, toward the dead engine.

You stop the yaw with rudder on the side of the good engine. The right pedal swings the rudder, pushing the tail left and the nose back right. Once the yaw has stopped, a little aileron banks the airplane toward the good engine.

On twins whose propellers both turn clockwise as seen from the cockpit, the left engine is the worse one to lose, the critical engine.

Why the rudder runs out as you slow

VMC is the slowest speed at which you can still keep the airplane straight after the critical engine suddenly fails. Most twins mark it with a red radial line near the low-speed end of the airspeed indicator, which pilots call the red line. It is not the never-exceed red line at the high-speed end.

The rudder works by turning the air past the tail, so its force shrinks as you slow. The good engine's yaw doesn't shrink. At full power a propeller pulls hardest at low speed.

At VMC, full rudder and a small bank toward the good engine only just hold the airplane straight.

A windmilling propeller, one the airflow keeps turning after its engine quits, makes it worse. At its worst it makes as much parasite drag, the drag of a shape pushing through the air, as the whole airframe. Feathering turns the blades edge-on to the wind and cuts that drag to a minimum.

The red line is one sea-level value, set with the conditions in the table at or near their worst. On the day, the real VMC can be lower or higher.

How a small bank helps the rudder

Bank toward the good engine and part of the wing's lift points sideways. That sideways part balances the rudder's sideways push. Without it, the rudder's push makes the airplane sideslip, moving partly sideways, toward the dead engine. That sideslip needs even more rudder, which raises VMC.

That is why the red line is set with up to 5 degrees of bank toward the good engine. Hold the wings level and VMC is much higher. In some twins you can lose control almost 20 knots above the red line.

For the best climb on one engine, fly zero sideslip, the air meeting the airplane head-on. That takes about 2 degrees of bank, with the ball in the slip indicator a third to a half out toward the good engine. That is less bank than the red line assumes, so the real VMC can rise above it.

How the swing becomes a roll

Below VMC, the yaw toward the dead engine swings the good engine's wing forward. That wing now travels faster than the other one.

The faster wing makes more lift than the slower one, so the twin rolls toward the dead engine.

Near the stall it gets worse. A yawing moment from one engine's thrust can start a spin, and no multiengine airplane is approved for spins.

Four mistakes that end in the roll

  • Put in rudder first, then aileron. The mistake is lifting the low wing with aileron alone. That adds drag on that side, pulling the nose further toward the dead engine. This adverse yaw grows as you slow.

  • Lower the nose to hold the blue line on the airspeed indicator, VYSE, the speed for the best climb on one engine. Keep the two-engine climb attitude and the airspeed bleeds toward the red line.

  • Pull the good engine's power back the moment the swing won't stop, or at the first sign of a stall. The mistake is keeping full power and raising the nose to save height. Lower the nose with it and accept the height loss.

  • Bring the power back in only at the blue line, with rudder for the yaw it brings. Adding it while slow starts the swing again. Then fly your checklist to identify, verify and feather the dead engine.

Check yourself

  1. Which rudder pedal do you press when your left engine quits in the climb and the nose swings left?

    Show the answerHide the answer

    The right pedal, on the good engine's side.

  2. What do you do when, near the red line with full right rudder in, the nose keeps swinging left?

    Show the answerHide the answer

    Pull the right engine's power back and lower the nose.

  3. Why could holding the wings level with the ball centered after an engine failure cost you control?

    Show the answerHide the answer

    With the wings level, no sideways lift helps the rudder, so VMC goes up.

  4. Do a heavier weight and a forward center of gravity raise VMC or lower it?

    Show the answerHide the answer

    They lower it, though too little bank can still push the real VMC above the red line.
