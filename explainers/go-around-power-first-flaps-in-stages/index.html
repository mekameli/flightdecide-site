Go-around: power first, flaps in stages

Home › Explainers › Go-around: power first, flaps in stages

The short version

Traffic on the runway, wind shear, a bigger airplane's wake or an unstable approach can each call for a go-around. You give up the landing and climb away. Cessna's checklist calls it a balked landing. A go-around is a normal maneuver. It only turns dangerous when it starts late or is flown wrong.

In a Cessna 172P go-around, power goes in first. Push the throttle full open, smoothly and without hesitation. The carb heat knob goes full in, to cold, so no heated air reaches the carburetor. Hold the nose with forward pressure and add right rudder.

Then bring the flaps up to 20 degrees and climb at 55 knots. Next comes 10 degrees until you're clear of obstacles, then all the way up at a safe height and 60 knots.

Power first, then hold the nose

Move the throttle smoothly, because a sudden shove can make some engines falter. Leave it full open. The 172P's go-around climb is flown at maximum power.

Carb heat was on for the approach, to keep ice out of the carburetor. Full heat at full throttle costs about 75 to 150 RPM, power you need now.

The sudden power tends to raise the nose and swing it left. This close to the ground, letting the nose rise too early could cause a stall you can't recover from.

So expect considerable forward pressure on the yoke to hold the nose level or in a safe climb attitude. You were trimmed for a slow approach, so the push is firm. Add right rudder to keep it straight.

Trim takes that pressure off the yoke. Once the speed and climb are set, give it a rough setting, then fine-tune it once things settle.

Flaps up in stages, and why

Flaps let the wing fly slower. Below its stall speed an airplane can't keep flying, and that speed is not fixed. Raise the flaps and it goes up, from about 33 knots with full flaps to about 44 with none.

Raise them all at once, just above the runway, and the sudden loss of lift can settle the airplane into the ground. Raised a little at a time, they give the airplane time to accelerate as each stage comes up.

Just above the runway, a stall leaves no room. In a 172P, recovering from one can cost as much as 230 feet.

The flap lever helps you stop at each stage. It has mechanical stops at 10 and 20 degrees.

A newer, fuel-injected 172S has no carb heat step and climbs at 60 knots. It raises the last flaps at a safe height and 65 knots. Fly your own airplane's checklist.

Four go-around mistakes to avoid

  • Add the power before you touch the flaps. Raise them all with the throttle at idle and the nose pulled up, and the airplane sinks instead of climbing.

  • Go around as soon as you need to. A late go-around usually comes from landing expectancy, the belief that the landing will work out, or from pride. Going around is never a failure.

  • Let the speed build before you pitch up. Near the ground the wing makes less drag, which is called ground effect. It can feel like a cushion of air, but that help is borrowed. Climb out of it too soon and you may be unable to climb or even hold altitude at full power.

  • In a very low go-around the airplane may settle onto the runway and bounce. Keep it straight and hold the pitch steady, rather than pulling the nose up. The power soon gives you a safe flying speed.
